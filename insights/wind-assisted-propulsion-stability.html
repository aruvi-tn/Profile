Stability first: retrofitting wind-assisted propulsion

Rotor sails, suction wings and rigid sails can cut fuel burn. They also add weight high above the deck and a new, persistent heeling moment. Here is what a retrofit has to prove before it sails.

Tamizharuvi Maniyan Annamalai · Lead Naval Architect5 min read

Why stability leads the retrofit

A wind-assisted ship propulsion (WASP) unit is tall, heavy and works by generating a large aerodynamic force. In stability terms that means three things at once: a higher VCG, more windage, and a heeling moment that exists whenever the system is producing thrust. Each is manageable; together they decide where the units can go and how the ship may be loaded.

1. Weight and centre of gravity

  • Each unit, its foundation and any deck reinforcement add to lightship — usually with a centre of gravity well above the main deck.
  • Raising the lightship VCG reduces GM in every loading condition. Conditions that were comfortably compliant may become marginal, especially light-ballast and arrival conditions.
  • After installation, class and flag will typically require a lightweight check or a full inclining experiment to confirm the new lightship, followed by an updated stability booklet.

2. Windage and the weather criterion

The IMO weather criterion compares the energy of a gusting beam wind against the ship’s righting energy. The wind heeling lever depends on the lateral projected area above the waterline and the height of its centroid:

lw1 = P · A · Z / (1000 · g · Δ) P — wind pressure (504 Pa) · A — lateral windage area (m²) · Z — lever from the centre of A to half draught (m) · Δ — displacement (t)

Tall rotors or wings add area very high up, so Z increases as well as A. The weather criterion often becomes the governing check after a WASP retrofit.

3. The operating heeling moment

Unlike passive windage, a WASP unit is designed to produce a large force. Part of it drives the ship forward; part of it heels the ship. The operating envelope needs its own checks:

  • Maximum operating heel under the highest system force, with adequate residual stability.
  • Stowed or survival condition — rotors stopped, wings feathered or lowered — under storm wind.
  • Failure cases — what happens if the system cannot be stopped or stowed.

Classification societies now publish specific rules or guidelines for wind-assisted propulsion, covering these load cases alongside structural and control-system requirements. Which set applies depends on the ship’s class.

4. Beyond stability

  • Foundations and fatigue — cyclic aerodynamic and inertial loads at the deck interface; local strength and fatigue assessment are part of the approval.
  • Navigation bridge visibility — tall units in front of the bridge can conflict with SOLAS visibility requirements and drive placement.
  • Air draft and cargo operations — bridges, cranes and port infrastructure.

A workable sequence

  • Concept placement against visibility, cargo and air-draft constraints.
  • Weight estimate and updated lightship; recompute all booklet loading conditions.
  • Intact and damage stability, weather criterion and WASP operating cases.
  • Structure and fatigue at the foundations; class submission and comment closure.
  • Installation, inclining or lightweight survey, and the final stability booklet with operating limits for the crew.
The fuel saving is the headline, but approval runs through stability. Getting the weight, windage and operating heel cases right early avoids moving a 30-metre structure on paper late in the project.
